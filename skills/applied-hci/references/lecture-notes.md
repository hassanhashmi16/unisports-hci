## Lecture-01 · Introduction to HCI · 45 slides

- **1–4.** Course orientation, assessment and class policy. The syllabus and assignment-specific Classroom post supply the deliverable requirements.
- **5–10.** Evaluate design in its context. The elevator's bottom-row symbols look like buttons, the vending machine requires a nonstandard first action, and the marble answering machine makes core operations tangible and short. The smart-TV examples compare remote-grid typing, swiping and speech. Choose the approach that fits users, tasks and environment.
- **11–24.** Interaction design supports communication and activity in everyday or work contexts. It brings together HCI, related disciplines, design practice and interdisciplinary work. Usability and experience both matter. Pragmatic qualities support the user's goals. Hedonic qualities shape stimulation and feeling. The iPod visual illustrates the joint role of simple interaction, pleasure, product identity and context. Study real users and evaluate their experience.
- **25–32.** Accessibility and inclusiveness concern different abilities, assistive methods, situations and cultures. Usability goals are effectiveness, efficiency, safety, utility, learnability and memorability. Experience goals describe desirable and undesirable feelings. Choose relevant goals and express what evidence would support them.
- **33–45.** Make relevant actions visible, provide feedback, use constraints and logical mapping, and keep behavior consistent. The hotel elevator's hidden card prerequisite calls for a visible reader and prompt. The connector labels work better adjacent to the ports, with color as reinforcement. Landmark telephone and calculator keypads have different domain conventions. Use perceived affordances and signifiers to show how digital controls behave.

## Lecture-02 · Computers and paradigms · 14 slides

- **1–3.** Input, output, special displays, physical interaction, paper, memory, processing and networks affect the experience. A new technology can change the assumed relationship between the user and the computer.
- **4–10.** The cumulative cartoons trace impersonal batch work, time-shared interaction, networked communities, graphical direct manipulation, personal computing, web-scale information and ubiquitous computing. The graphical example swaps recalled file commands for action on visible objects. Treat these as interaction paradigms suited to different tasks and devices.
- **11–14.** Agent interfaces return to language with more proactive interpretation. WIMP supports action on a representation of the user's world. Understand the work people are doing, then choose the interaction style and feedback that fit it.

## Lecture-03 · The human · 42 slides

- **1–14.** Vision involves reception and interpretation. Size and depth depend on visual angle, acuity and context. The Ponzo and Müller-Lyer diagrams demonstrate how surrounding cues alter apparent lengths. Reading uses fixations and saccades. Sound offers frequency, amplitude and timbre cues. Touch conveys pressure, temperature, pain, and feedback alongside kinesthetic awareness. Reaction and movement times differ across stimuli and users. The Fitts relationship favors nearer, wider targets.
- **15–27.** Sensory buffers include visual, aural and tactile memory. Attention and rehearsal connect sensory, working and long-term memory. Chunking and meaningful structure aid learning. Episodic and semantic memory play different roles. Dog-family networks, frames, a vet-visit script and conditional rules illustrate how knowledge is represented. Retrieval by recognition has the cue present. Recall needs more support. Use meaningful groups and contextual reminders.
- **28–37.** Deduction derives implications from premises. Induction generalizes from observations. Abduction proposes a cause for an observation. The Wason task tests the vowel and odd-number cards because either can falsify the rule. Gestalt restructuring, a problem space with legal operators and analogical mapping help explain problem solving. Skilled behavior chunks information. A slip is faulty execution with the right intention. A mistake originates in the goal or understanding.
- **38–42.** Emotion involves physiological and cognitive factors. Stress and negative affect narrow problem solving, while positive affect can promote flexibility. Account for lasting differences, temporary fatigue and changing abilities. Context matters when carrying historical experimental values into a real interface.

## Lecture-04 · Cognitive aspects · 69 slides

- **1–17.** Experiential/reflective and fast/slow thinking help explain differences across tasks. Cognitive processes overlap. In the hotel-rate activity, the tabular display averaged 3.2 seconds versus 5.5 for the denser-looking listing with comparable information density. Organized columns and headings aid selective attention. Multitasking, interruptions and driving examples illustrate attention limits. Make information salient when relevant to the current task.
- **18–23.** The Italian/French findability examples compare alternating color with borders and white space. The grouping with borders is easier to scan in the cited comparison. The contrast samples show that color-on-color and texture can obscure words. Make icons distinguishable, text legible and sounds discriminable.
- **24–39.** Encoding and retrieval depend on attention and context. People often recognize visual cues more readily than recalling arbitrary facts. Miller's working-memory result does not set a menu item limit. Displayed items can be scanned. Personal information management involves keeping, organizing and retrieving items. Spotlight, authentication memory load, digital forgetting and SenseCam illustrate search, burden, control over reminders, and external memory aids.
- **40–47.** Learning may be intentional or incidental. Offer safe exploration and contextual guidance, with linked representations when helpful. Support reading, speaking and listening according to ability and task. Keep spoken instructions short and give access to alternative formats. Provide help and intelligible choices for reflective tasks. Treat reliance on apps in the dilemma as a discussion question.
- **48–58.** A mental model is learned through use and shapes prediction. The thermostat example exposes an erroneous more-is-faster model, and repeated elevator presses can signal poor feedback. Make outcomes interpretable, give contextual explanations and use transparent controls. The gulfs diagram asks how to act and how to tell the current state. The information-processing figure shows encoding, comparison, response selection and execution. It simplifies the social and material context.
- **59–69.** Distributed cognition spans people, tools and representations, as shown by the cockpit and air-traffic-control coordination diagram. External representations include task lists, reminders, annotations and movable artifacts. Computational offloading reduces mental operations. Embodied interaction concerns meaning formed through physical and social engagement. The contactless-payment activity elicits mental models and checks them against system behavior.

## Lecture-05 · Interaction models and ergonomics · 24 slides

- **1–9.** Distinguish domain, user goal, task and operations. Norman's cycle runs from goal, intention, action specification and execution through state perception, interpretation and evaluation. The font-size table turns each stage into an observable step and checks whether the outcome met the goal.
- **10–16.** Diagnose the gulfs and separate slips from mistakes. Norman describes the user perspective. Abowd and Beale add translations between user, input, system and output. Articulation maps task language to input, performance maps input to core operations, presentation maps core state to output, and observation maps output back to user understanding. The shortcut, spelling, installation and dialog examples locate problems in specific translations.
- **17–24.** Ergonomics links controls, displays and physical context. Group by function, workflow sequence or frequency. Use color and contrast to reinforce meaning, as the red-on-red terms example illustrates. Consider workspace, posture, lighting, temperature, noise and fatigue when relevant to the task.

## Lecture-06 · Interaction styles and WIMP · 27 slides

- **1–11.** Choose among commands, menus, natural language, query interfaces, form fills, WIMP, point-and-click and 3D styles by goal and expertise. Commands can accelerate skilled repetition. Menus support recognition. Guided booking queries demonstrate a constrained path for novices. Use meaningful field structure, clear correction and predictable defaults.
- **12–22.** Windows group independent views, icons represent objects/actions, pointers have hotspots, and menus reveal available operations. The screenshots compare menu bars, drop-down, pop-up and pie menus. Keep menu language and groups meaningful. Radio buttons indicate mutually exclusive selections and checkboxes independent selections. Toolbars surface frequent actions. Dialogs ask focused questions or show necessary information.
- **23–27.** Evaluate the interaction's look and feel through actual back-and-forth. Speech and dialog systems need intelligible context, recognition feedback and repair. The travel-booking conversation shows how a clarification can recover from a wrong destination without restarting.

## Lecture-07 · Interaction design lifecycle · 20 slides

- **1–6.** Discover requirements, design alternatives, prototype and evaluate. User-, activity- and systems-centered approaches put emphasis on different units of analysis. Genius design relies more on designer expertise. The Double Diamond depicts divergent discovery and convergent definition of the problem, then divergent development and convergent delivery. Test assumptions about the problem space early.
- **7–13.** User involvement supports expectation management and ownership. Participation can be ongoing, scheduled, online, crowdsourced or post-release. User-centered design combines early focus on users and tasks, empirical measurement and iteration. The lifecycle arrows allow revisiting stages. The sprint figure progresses from unpack, sketch and decide to prototype and test. Research in the Wild links theory, technology, design and in-situ study.
- **14–20.** Separate stakeholders from direct users and learn the goals behind requests. Generate real alternatives from research, cross-disciplinary input and related domains. Choose using observable behavior, feasibility, user feedback and thresholds. A/B testing needs comparable groups and an appropriate metric. Agile integration supports frequent feedback and change when coupled with research.

## Lecture-08 · Data gathering · 27 slides

- **1–4.** Plan goals and analysis, participant selection, the participant relationship, triangulation and a pilot. Distinguish population from sample. Convenience and snowball sampling affect what can be inferred. Triangulate across sources, investigators, theory or methods. Use proportionate notes, audio, video or images with suitable consent.
- **5–9.** Use structured interviews for comparability, unstructured for discovery, semi-structured for both, and focus groups for multiple perspectives. Avoid long, compound, leading and jargon-heavy questions. Introduce the study and permission, warm up, ask core questions, cool down and close. Remote methods change rapport and access. The group visual shows how a prototype or scenario can prompt discussion.
- **10–17.** Order surveys deliberately, keep instructions and response categories clear, choose open, closed, Likert or semantic formats to match the claim, and pilot the completed form. Online distribution speeds collection while introducing sampling and duplicate-response issues. The scale visual contrasts verbal poles and numeric response positions.
- **18–27.** Compare direct field observation, direct controlled observation and indirect diaries/logs. Structure observations around person, place and activity, or use details such as actors, space, objects, actions, events, time, goals and feelings. Ethnography involves immersion and iterative interpretation. Think-aloud reveals reasoning in controlled sessions. The comparison table pairs the method with its data, advantages and limitations. Combine methods where corroboration matters.

## Lecture-09 · Discovering requirements · 31 slides

- **1–7.** Requirements say what the product should do or how it should perform. The tire-swing cartoon shows stakeholder miscommunication. Volere's annotated example includes source, rationale, measurable fit criterion, priority and related evidence. The seven-dimensions visual distinguishes user, interface, action, data, control, environment and quality attributes.
- **8–14.** Consider functional, data, environmental/context, user, usability and UX requirements. Context includes physical, social, organizational and technical conditions. Novices may need prompts, experts accelerators, and casual users visible paths. Pair interviews, surveys and observations with documentation and comparable products. Workable security matters because excessive burden encourages workarounds.
- **15–19.** Cultural, design, technology and provocative probes prompt useful behavior and reflection. Contextual inquiry is a user-as-expert field interview using context, partnership, interpretation and focus. Interpret ambiguous behaviors together. The joy-of-life and joy-of-use prompts explore broader and immediate effects. Brainstorm with varied viewpoints, a clear focus and a record of alternatives.
- **20–26.** Profiles summarize relevant characteristics, and personas synthesize those patterns as plausible user archetypes. The Ginnie and family-traveler cards show different ways to present goals, motivations, background and frustrations. The family sailing scenario connects multiple users, devices, preferences and resumption to a task. The runner visual separates persona, scenario and goal. Treat personality scores or decorative attributes as meaningful only when supported by research.
- **27–31.** Essential use cases pair user intent with system responsibility. A fuller use case maps numbered interactions and alternatives. The visa example branches on invalid destination/nationality and unavailable information. Trace evidence → requirement → scenario/use case → flow → evaluation.

## Lecture-10 · Research ethics · 11 slides

- **1–4.** Explain the purpose, recordings, data use and right to withdraw. Minimize data, protect identities and store it securely. The anonymization table generalizes identifying fields into groups and shows why combinations of data matter.
- **5–7.** Deception needs a defensible reason, minimal harm, no workable alternative and debriefing. The A/B comparison shows split audiences and different conversion outcomes. The Wizard-of-Oz picture separates the participant/facilitator from the hidden operator. Account for what participants were led to believe.
- **8–11.** Take additional care with participants in vulnerable or dependent positions. Use accessible consent, appropriate assent and parental consent for children, non-coercive incentives and voluntary participation. Check reuse and ownership before secondary use. Consider who the sample excludes and the bias that may introduce. The course take-home prompt also references ethical and legal issues by gathering method.

## Lecture-11 · Design, prototyping and construction · 33 slides

- **1–6.** Prototypes are representations stakeholders can interact with and evaluate. The PalmPilot model and 3D-print examples show that the prototype's medium depends on the question. Use prototypes to compare alternatives and communicate through behavior.
- **7–13.** Low fidelity can use sketches, paper, indexed cards, storyboards and Wizard of Oz. The sketches use simple symbols, one scene per step, and cards with distinct controls. High fidelity resembles the intended experience. Horizontal fidelity is broad and shallow. Vertical fidelity is narrow and deep. Label compromises and select the scope by what needs testing.
- **14–20.** Conceptual design specifies concepts, functions, relationships, data and responsibility. Concrete design sets UI details, accessibility, localization and input/output. The Guard Llama mood board aligns desired feel. Compare interface metaphors by relevance, structure, representability, audience understanding and extensibility. Consider instructing, conversing, manipulating, exploring and responding.
- **21–26.** Derive storyboard frames from scenario steps and cards from scenario/use-case interactions. The two sets of visa cards show equivalent choices with different prompts and labels. The journey wheel and timeline show multiple representations of before/during/after experience, actions, questions, comments and opportunities. Use the one that makes evidence and sequence clearest.
- **27–33.** The Arduino, MaKey MaKey and micro:bit visuals illustrate physical prototyping toolkits. SDK examples illustrate shortcuts to platform capabilities. Consider these only where the approved project needs them. Carry the conceptual decisions into concrete design and evaluate before engineering deeper functionality.

## Lecture-12 · Interaction design basics and local navigation · 33 slides

- **1–8.** Design the whole interaction in context. Design reaches a goal within constraints and involves trade-offs. Understand both people and computer capabilities. The design-process diagram links discovery, analysis, design and implementation with prototyping and evaluation. Investigate how the design made a human error likely.
- **9–19.** Talk to and observe users. Build a research-grounded persona and let the details inform decisions, as Betty's work context illustrates. The film scenario makes actions, thoughts and system behavior concrete. Walk through what the user sees, does and thinks. A scenario describes one path, so explore alternatives in separate scenarios or a flow.
- **20–24.** Distinguish widgets, screen-local layout, application navigation, and wider platform or ecosystem interactions. The visual pairs a local goal path with a site hierarchy and a network diagram. Select the representation that answers the design question.
- **25–33.** The goal-path series illustrates progress, overshooting and backtracking when users rely on local cues alone. Help users determine where they are, what they can do, what will happen, and where they have been or what they have done. The breadcrumb screenshot illustrates location and live ancestors. The download-page and oversized ambiguous-button examples show why prominence needs meaningful wording.

## Lecture-13 · Global navigation and screen layout · 27 slides

- **1–7.** Hierarchies organize screens and capabilities by function. Map the user's vocabulary and avoid excessive depth. The stock-control example shows why users need task-appropriate links between stock, outstanding orders and customers across branches. Working-memory findings do not set a navigation breadth limit.
- **8–13.** A dialog has a repeatable interaction pattern with different instance details, as the ceremony example illustrates. Network diagrams show action order, transitions, branches and confirmation paths. Consider cross-application links, familiar platform behavior and standard operations such as copy and paste.
- **14–19.** Form follows function. Group logically related items physically, as the billing, delivery and order sketch demonstrates. Order items by natural task, then match keyboard tab sequence. Put preparatory steps before the actions that need them. Use boxes and typography sparingly to make groupings clear.
- **20–27.** Align by what the user scans for. Align continuous left-to-right text at its leading edge, make surname lookup easy where appropriate, right-align integers or align decimal points to aid comparisons, and help users scan across columns with leaders, subtle striping or carefully chosen alignment. The sweets table and unequal decimal strings make these trade-offs concrete.

## Lecture-14 · Whitespace, form design and iteration · 26 slides

- **1–7.** Use whitespace as a structural device. The block diagrams show separation between peers, closer spacing within a group and isolation to highlight an item. Avoid gaps that visually detach related components.
- **8–12.** The same microwave panel is annotated five ways to demonstrate grouping, order, decoration, alignment and whitespace. Related defrost and time controls cluster. The step sequence ends with Start. Borders, color and gaps reinforce relationships. Centered labels still need to be easy to scan.
- **13–18.** Place input and labels in natural task and reading order. Design the keyboard traversal and distinguish active controls from static content. The mug handle illustrates a physical affordance. In software, familiar appearances and labels signal possible actions. Choose sorting, diagrams or tables by the information task and use appropriate interactivity.
- **19–22.** The textured background undermines legibility and the 3D pie distorts comparisons. Use restrained decoration and redundant color cues, and inspect grayscale readability. Localization includes language, ordering, symbols, dates and other conventions. Internationalization makes local equivalents possible.
- **23–26.** Design, prototype, evaluate and revise. Formative work makes the design better, and summative work checks criteria near the end. The hills diagram shows local improvements can trap a team in a weak concept. Return to the underlying problem or consider another starting point when symptoms persist.

## Usability Goals · Task performance and experience · 19 slides

- **1–8.** Define effectiveness, efficiency, safety, utility, learnability and memorability in the task context. The comparison exercise also scores satisfaction. Interview, observe, use think-aloud and rate perceptions with well-defined scales. A rating is numerical evidence about a subjective experience.
- **15–19.** The product scorecard, quantitative example table and worksheet compare goals, measurement choices and baseline or target priorities. The visual list pairs desirable experiences such as helpfulness and enjoyment with unwanted ones such as frustration or feeling patronized. Choose goals relevant to the product and acknowledge tensions between them. ## Lecture-15 · Shneiderman and Norman's seven principles · 24 slides

- **1–6.** Shneiderman's first two rules cover consistency and universal usability, including shortcuts for frequently performed tasks. The Office shortcuts and desktop/mobile interface comparisons illustrate consistency in behavior across tools and form factors. The keyboard and Photoshop chart show accelerators for learned tasks.
- **7–14.** Offer informative feedback, give task closure, prevent errors and support simple handling, permit reversal, keep users in control and reduce short-term memory load. Visuals show progress when copying, a successful upload state, collision options, undo, meaningful cancel choices and breadcrumbs. Provide proportionate feedback for actions of different significance. The illustrated checklist turns the eight rules into review prompts.
- **15–19.** Norman's seven principles are to use knowledge in the world and in the head, simplify tasks, make relevant things visible, get mappings right, exploit natural and artificial constraints, design for error, and standardize when needed. Bike-rack examples externalize what belongs where. The sign-up form compresses a task. The two gulfs visuals connect perception and action to goal achievement.
- **20–24.** The on/off controls and status lights connect a change to perceptible state. Stovetop layouts and the Caps Lock cue show natural or adjacent mapping. The window and table examples constrain invalid or unavailable operations. The animated sign-in example places a wrong-password message beside the affected field and keeps a recovery option visible. Design understandable recovery and standardize controls whose behavior cannot be made self-evident.

## Lecture-20 part i · Norman's six design principles · 15 slides

- **1–6.** Visibility makes relevant state/actions findable. Comparing a hamburger menu with a tab bar shows that immediate discoverability and available space can trade off. Feedback communicates what happened, with visual, auditory or other channels as appropriate.
- **7–10.** Constraints limit possible actions and guide users. Physical, logical and cultural constraints can aid interpretation. The spreadsheet warning illustrates a disallowed operation. Stove knob placement, the Control Center comparison and the Caps Lock light illustrate mapping between control and effect.
- **11–15.** Keep internal and external consistency in terminology, interaction and appearance. Desktop and phone variants retain familiar patterns while adapting layout. The turn/pull/push door comparison illustrates how perceived shape and clues suggest actions. Use familiar control appearance, with text when a clue alone is ambiguous.

## Lecture-20 part ii · Theo Mandel's golden rules · 14 slides

- **1–6.** Put users in control through modeless interaction where appropriate, flexible input, interruptibility, descriptive messages, immediate and reversible actions, navigable paths and exits, accessibility, direct manipulation, personalization and a UI that stays out of the way when appropriate. Examples include preserving a draft when interrupted, Undo Send and drag-and-drop alongside discoverable control paths.
- **7–10.** Reduce memory load with short-term memory aids, recognition, visual cues, helpful defaults, undo/redo, shortcuts for frequent actions, object-action syntax, familiar metaphors, progressive disclosure and visual clarity. The cart summary, thumbnails and contextual command examples retain relevant information in view.
- **11–14.** Make the interface consistent with task continuity, platform and product-family conventions, predictable action results, coherent aesthetics and safe exploration. Stable navigation, familiar back/save behavior and visible undo turn consistency into something users can rely on. Evidence in the deck is organized under the three pillars, with some techniques serving multiple pillars.

## Evaluation Metrics · Fitts' Law · 12 slides

- **1–5.** A predictive model estimates a skilled pointing movement. Distance and effective width along the approach influence acquisition time. The diagrams compare vertical and horizontal approaches and define width along the path. See the [evaluation reference](project-evaluation.md) for notation and conditions.
- **6–10.** Screen-edge and corner controls benefit when a mouse cannot overshoot. The Windows taskbars illustrate the cost of a one-pixel gap. A mini toolbar near the cursor reduces distance. The pie and linear menu examples contrast reach and geometry. The phone graphic and short-dropdown example illustrate nearby targets, with actual reach depending on hand, grip and layout.
- **11–12.** Evaluate the actionable hit area, spacing and start position. Size and position controls to support frequent selection. Measure in the intended device and decision context. Edge effects depend on the interaction environment and don't generalize to arbitrary containers or touch by default.

## Lecture-23 · Universal design · 27 slides

- **1–5.** Universal design seeks usable experiences for a wide range of people and situations from the start. Small print, caps, handed scissors and fixed seating show how a default can exclude people. The concentric diagram rejects a single average user across ability, size, context, age, language and culture.
- **6–11.** Consider visual, auditory, physical, speech, dyslexia and autism-related needs, and differences in age and culture. Use screen-reader semantics, zoomable text, alternatives to color, captions/transcripts, visual or haptic alternatives, flexible input and usable targets. The color-vision comparison shows why error and success need labels, shape or position cues. The tap-target graphic distinguishes platform guidance and units. Give the audience relevant language, direction, date, number and icon conventions.
- **12–19.** The seven principles are equitable use, flexibility in use, simple and intuitive use, perceptible information, tolerance for error, low physical effort, and size and space for approach and use. Automatic doors illustrate shared access. The media controls show icons with labels. Visual, audio and tactile cues demonstrate redundancy. Prevent, warn and recover illustrate tolerance for error. Account for comfortable reach and fatigue.
- **20–27.** Multimodal interaction offers more than one way to act or perceive relevant state. Multimedia presents multiple media types. Consider speech units and prosody in voice interactions. Auditory icons use recognizable sounds, earcons use learned motifs, and sonification maps data to sound. The motif-plus-modifier example conveys action and object. The homework uses visually impaired children as a specific research population.

## Lecture-24 · Design Thinking and AgileUX · 16 slides

- **1–8.** Design Thinking is human-centered inquiry for complex, ill-defined problems. The five modes are empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test, with arrows that permit reframing and return visits. The overlapping lenses distinguish desirability, feasibility and viability. Start with real human needs and use prototypes to learn.
- **9–12.** Agile values people and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change. The Scrum figure traces product backlog, sprint backlog, development and demonstration. AgileUX combines iterative software delivery with interaction design research, prototypes and evaluation using a suitable rhythm.
- **13–16.** The comparison visuals identify iteration, user focus, collaboration and adaptation as shared traits. Design Thinking emphasizes discovering the problem and shaping an approach. Agile emphasizes incremental delivery and refinement. Protect discovery, research and reflection while iterating on the approved product.
